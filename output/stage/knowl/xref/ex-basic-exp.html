Example 4.3.1.

We’ll again consider Pete and his move into his house in July 2016. When he moved in, there were three bunny rabbits living under the big tree. He’s noticed that that number of bunny rabbits appears to be doubling each year. Determine a model for the bunny rabbit population.
🔗
Solution.
As always in a modeling problem, we start by defining our variables. It is common to use \(P\) for a population variable and \(t\) for a time variable, so we’ll do that. Define \(P\) to be the number of rabbits living under the tree and \(t\) to be the number of years since July 2016. The initial value of the population, when \(t = 0\) is three rabbits, so \(P_0 = 3\text{.}\)
🔗
The common rate sounds simple here, but dealing with percentages always has the potential to be tricky. If a quantity doubles, it is increasing by 100%. So, the common rate, \(r\) is 100% and as a decimal that is \(r = 1\text{.}\) In our general formula, the base of the exponential is \(1 + r\text{,}\) the common multiplier, and that here is equal to 2 … and that probably feels more correct than thinking about the rate being 100%.
🔗
Putting all that together gives a final formula of
\begin{equation*} P = P_0(1 + r)^t = 3(1 + 1)^t = 3(2)^t. \end{equation*}
