    Section 5.2 Categorical Data

    We said in Section 5.1, that the methods of descriptive statistics allow us to summarize data. Recall our question about if you (who gets up early) or your roommate (who sleeps until the afternoon) are more typical? We considered how we’d keep track of the data in a survey to try to study this question; probably a chart. In Example 5.1.3, only a few rows of the chart are shown. We need to summarize! In this section, we’ll focus on the data for the categorical varibles.
    🔗
    This (completely made up) data is in a spreadsheet that you can generate your own copy of with this link. You can use
    🔗
    We’ll start with the simplest thing, and the most relevant data to the initial question, “What proportion of college students are morning people?”
    🔗

    Example 5.2.1. Frequency Table.

    The following chart is called a frequency table.
    🔗
    Response Frequency Relative Frequency
    Proportion    		 Relative Frequency
    Percentage
    Yes 94 0.376 37.6%
    No 156 0.624 62.4%
    TOTAL 250 1 100%
    With a binary variable, the chart is small. If you were presenting this, you might rather just say, “Out of the 250 students surveyed, 94 (or 37.6%) consider themselves a morning person.” For another categorical variable with more options, a chart helps keep all the information organized.
    🔗
    🔗
    The frequency column represents a count of how many times a particular response appears. This should always be a whole number. Both of the relative frequency columns put that count into the context of the whole. It is a good idea to have both in your table. You can revisit Section 3.1 to review the details of the percentage formula and the pros and cons of both absolute and relative change. The total of the frequency column should always be the sample size. The relative frequency column should total to 1 or 100%, but can sometimes be a little bit off because of rounding.
    🔗

    Example 5.2.2.

    Generate a frequency table for the various majors of the students in the sample.
    🔗
    Hint.
    You can use the COUNTIF function in Google Sheets to get the frequency. Typing “=countif(G2:G251,"Game Design")” into a cell and pressing enter will produce 17, the number of students in the sample with a Game Design major. This particular function isn’t covered in Section 1.5, but a review of that section will likely help you figure out how to modify this to count whatever you want.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We can use the COUNTIF function in the spreadsheet to give us the frequency for each response. To compute the relative frequency, divide the frequency by 250. TO write the relative frequency as a percentage, move to decimal to the right two places.
    🔗
    Response Frequency Relative Frequency
    Proportion    		 Relative Frequency
    Percentage
    Nursing 54 0.216 21.6%
    Business Administration 39 0.156 15.6%
    Communications Media 24 0.096 9.6%
    Criminal Justice 23 0.092 9.2%
    Psychology 21 0.084 8.4%
    Game Design 17 0.068 6.8%
    Biology 15 0.06 6%
    Interdisciplinary Studies 11 0.044 4.4%
    Exercise & Sport Science 11 0.044 4.4%
    Engineering Technology 10 0.04 4%
    Human Services 10 0.04 4%
    Education 9 0.036 3.6%
    Other 6 0.024 2.4%
    TOTAL 250 1 100%
    The last row, “Other”, groups together the four majors (Computer Science, English Studies, History, and Mathematics) that have particularly low frequencies. This is a choice you can make. It hides a bit of information, but turns four rows in the chart into a single row.

    A frequency table can only be used to summarize the data for a single variable. Often, we want to know something about how two variables are related. In the case of two categorical variables, we can do this in a two-way table.
    🔗

    Example 5.2.4. Two-Way Table.

    The following two-way table compares the whether a student considers themselves a morning person with the number of morning classes they are taking. The number in a box counts the number of responses that match the value of both the row and column variable.
    🔗
    Not a Morning Person Morning Person Row Totals
    0 Morning Classes 28 17 45
    1 Morning Class 29 21 50
    2 Morning Classes 29 27 56
    3 Morning Classes 36 21 57
    4 Morning Classes 34 8 42
    Column Totals 156 94 250
    In this case, we’re treating discrete numerical data as ordinal categorical data. We can always do this, but it may not always be a great idea. If there are too many options, so that a resulting table would have too many rows or columns, we can group values together.
