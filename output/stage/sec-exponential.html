    Section 4.3 Exponential Models

    In this section, we’ll explore the same types of questions and approaches to answering those questions as we did in the previous section, Section 4.2, but this time with exponential relationships. The techniques and formulas are only good if we’re applying them in the correct situation. So, as a review of what we saw in Section 4.1, a relationship is exponential if the RELATIVE change is the same between consecutive stages, and we call this relative change the common rate and denote it with \(r\text{.}\) In the explicit formula, this common rate is part of the base of the exponential, but just like linear relationships, we need some information about where to start, not just how the quantity in question changes. We use the same notation for our generic formula as we did with linear, \(y_0\) is the starting or initial value, but you’re encouraged to use a notation that works well for you and makes sense with the scenario.
    🔗
    As with linear relationships, determining the formula is the most straight forward when these two needed pieces of information are given to us directly.
    🔗

    Example 4.3.1.

    We’ll again consider Pete and his move into his house in July 2016. When he moved in, there were three bunny rabbits living under the big tree. He’s noticed that that number of bunny rabbits appears to be doubling each year. Determine a model for the bunny rabbit population.
    🔗
    Solution.
    As always in a modeling problem, we start by defining our variables. It is common to use \(P\) for a population variable and \(t\) for a time variable, so we’ll do that. Define \(P\) to be the number of rabbits living under the tree and \(t\) to be the number of years since July 2016. The initial value of the population, when \(t = 0\) is three rabbits, so \(P_0 = 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The common rate sounds simple here, but dealing with percentages always has the potential to be tricky. If a quantity doubles, it is increasing by 100%. So, the common rate, \(r\) is 100% and as a decimal that is \(r = 1\text{.}\) In our general formula, the base of the exponential is \(1 + r\text{,}\) the common multiplier, and that here is equal to 2 … and that probably feels more correct than thinking about the rate being 100%.
    🔗
    Putting all that together gives a final formula of
    \begin{equation*} P = P_0(1 + r)^t = 3(1 + 1)^t = 3(2)^t. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Again, we have found in Example 4.3.1 the explicit formula, but can also write down the recursive formula:
    \begin{align*} P_0 \amp = 3\\ P_n \amp = (1 + r)P_{n-1} = 2P_{n-1}. \end{align*}
    This form can make the idea of doubling at each stage more clear than in the explicit formula. But as before, the explicit form is often more practical when using the model formula to answer further questions about the scenario.
    🔗

    Example 4.3.2.

    For the same scenario as Example 4.3.1,
    1. How many bunny rabbits will be under Pete’s tree at the start of 2027? (Assume bunnieslive forever!)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. When will there be 42 bunnies?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We’ll follow the steps of Procedure 4.2.3 to answer these two questions.
    🔗
    For (a), we’ve already done Step 1 in Example 4.2.2, \(t = 10.5\text{.}\) We substitute that value into the explicit formula from Example 4.3.1 and then evaluate:
    \begin{align*} P \amp = 3(2)^t\\ \amp = 3(2)^{10.5}\\ \amp \approx 3(1448.155)\\ \amp \approx 4344.464 \end{align*}
    Notice that we first evaluated the exponential (using a calculator) BEFORE multiplying by 3. We’ve also indicated where we’ve rounded our result by using a \(\approx\) rather than an equal sign. For the final step in the procedure, we translate this back into the meaning in the scenario. Since we shouldn’t have partial bunnies, we’ll say that at the start of 2027, the model predicts there will be 4,344 bunny rabbits under Pete’s tree.
    🔗
    PLEASE NOTICE that this scenario is completely bananas; bunnies do not live forever. We’ll see later that exponential models tend to lose their accuracy over time as well.
    🔗
    The procedure works just as well for part (b), but Step 3 involves using logarithms. If you need a review, visit Section 2.6 now.
    🔗
    The first two steps of the procedure give the following equation with the single unknown variable, \(t\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 42 = 3(2)^t. \end{equation*}
    We follow the process of Procedure 2.6.8 to solve the exponential equation; one where the unknown quantity is in the exponent.
    🔗
    1. We isolate the exponential by dividing both sides of the equation by 3:
      \begin{align*} \frac{42}{3} \amp = \frac{3(2)^t}{3}\\ 14 \amp = 2^t \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We make sure when we apply the logarithm it is to EVERYTHING on BOTH sides of the equation:
      \begin{align*} \log(14) \amp = \log\left(2^t\right) \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. We apply the exponent property of logs:
      \begin{equation*} \log(14) = t \log(2) \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    4. We solve for the variable by dividing both sides by \(\log(2)\text{:}\)
      \begin{align*} \frac{log(14)}{\log(2)} \amp = \frac{t\log(2)}{\log(2)}\\ \frac{log(14)}{\log(2)} \amp = t \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    We use a calculator to do the final computation:
    \begin{equation*} t = \frac{\log(14)}{\log(2)} \approx 3.8. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Lastly, we determine what this value means in our scenario. Since we’ve found a non-whole number answer, we can choose how exact we want to get. The answer of 3.8 is between 3 and 4, so sometime between July 2019 and July 2020 the \(42^{\text{nd}}\) bunny was born. We could convert .8 years into a number of months using the skills from Section 3.2:
    \begin{equation*} 0.8\text{yr} \cdot \frac{12\text{mo}}{1\text{yr}} = 9.6\text{mo}. \end{equation*}
    About 9 and a half months after July 1, 2019 is around mid-April in the year 2020. We could go further and convert 0.8 years into days to determine the exact date, but population models are rarely that accurate to begin with; especially when we’re pretending bunnies live forever.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Check for Understanding 4.3.3.

    The population of a certain city was 299000 in 2018, and the observed relative growth rate is 2 percent per year.
    🔗
    1. Let \(t\) be the number of years after 2018 and \(r\) be the relative growth rate. Find a function that models the population after \(t\) years.
      🔗
      \(P(t)=\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the projected population in the year 2027:
      🔗
      🔗
    3. In what year will the population reach 418672?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer 1.
    \(299000\cdot 1.02^{t}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer 2.
    \(357333\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer 3.
    \(2035\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    a. The model will be an exponential model: \(P(t) = a(1+r)^t\text{,}\) where the initial population will be \(a=229000\) and growth rate as \(r=2/100 = 0.02\text{.}\) The model then is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(t) = 299000\ast1.02^t \end{equation*}
    🔗
    b. To find the projected population, first note that 2027 is \(t=9\) years after 2018. This value of \(t\) will be substituted into the model so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(9) = 299000\ast1.02^{9} = {357333} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    c. Use logarithms to solve the equation \(\displaystyle{{418672}=299000\ast 1.02^t }\) for \(t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(\displaystyle{\begin{aligned} {418672}\amp =299000\ast 1.02^t \\ {418672}/299000 \amp = 1.02^t \\ {1.40024} \amp = 1.02^t \\ \ln({1.40024}) \amp = \ln\big(1.02^t\big)=t \ln(1.02) \\ t \amp = \frac{\ln({1.40024})}{\ln(1.02)}\\ t \amp = {17} \end{aligned}}\)
    🔗
    So the year is \(2018+t =2035\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We won’t always be given an initial value and consistant growth rate to simply put in the proper place in our formula. The next problem is an example of that. We still work with the same format for an exponential relationship. If we’re careful with what each piece represents and the methods used to solve for variables in different places, we’ll be just fine. Hopefully we can see that this is most definitely a longer process, but not really any different from the more straightforward examples we’ve seen.
    🔗

    Example 4.3.4.

    The number of users on a social networking site was 45,000 in February when they officially went public, and grew to 60,000 by October. If the number of the site users is growing exponentially, and growth continues at the same rate, how many users should they expect two years after they went public? When would the number of users exceed a million?
    🔗
    Solution.
    We will need an explicit formula to efficiently answer both of the these questions. We’ll start by defining our variables. The output variable is the number of users of the site. Since those are given in thousands, we might as well use that too. So, let \(U\) respresent the number of thousands of users of the site. The input variable is some sort of time element. Since this is given in months, we’ll use that too. Thus, \(t\) will represent the number of months since the site went public.
    🔗
    There are two things we need to figure out in order to write an exponential formula to model any situation: an initial value and a consistent percentage change. The initial value is the value of the output variable (in this case \(U\)) when the input variable (\(t\) here) is 0. When \(t = 0\text{,}\) we’re talking about February when the site went public; there were 45,000 users, so \(U_0=45\text{.}\) (The subscript of 0 is how we indicate that this is the initial value and we’re using 45 rather than 45,000 since we’ve made the units for the \(U\) variable thousands of people.)
    🔗
    The rate is a little trickier in this example. It is not given to us directly in words and the two data points we have are separated by a time interval of 8 months. In October (of the same year the site went public), \(t=8\text{,}\) and the site has 60,000 users so \(U = 60\text{.}\) We can substitute these values into the general format of the formula:
    \begin{equation*} 60 = 45(1 + r)^8. \end{equation*}
    Now, we have an equation with only one unknown variable, so we can solve for \(r\text{.}\) Check out Section 2.5 for a refresher if you need.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} 60 \amp = 45(1+r)^8\\ \frac{60}{45} \amp = \frac{45(1+r)^8}{45}\\ 1.333 \amp = (1 + r)^8\\ \sqrt[8]{1.333} \amp = \sqrt[8]{(1+r)^8}\\ 1.037 \amp = 1 + r\\ 0.037 \amp = r\\ 3.7\% \amp = r \end{align*}
    🔗
    This means that the number of users is growing at a rate of 3.7% each month, and the final explicit formula is:
    \begin{equation*} U = 45(1.037)^t. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We follow Procedure 4.2.3 for both questions. First we consider how many users the site has two years after going public. Since our input variable, \(t\text{,}\) is measured in months, we need to consider how many months two years later is. There are 12 months in a year, so two years after going public, \(t = 24\text{.}\) (That’s Step 1.) Now, we can substitute and evaluate (Steps 2 and 3):
    \begin{align*} U \amp = 45(1.037)^t\\ \amp = 45(1.037)^{24}\\ \amp = 45(2.392)\\ \amp = 107.622 \end{align*}
    In Step 4, we translate our numeric answer back into words that connect to the scenario. Since the units for \(U\) are thousands of people, this means after two years of being public the cite has 107,622 users.
    🔗
    Answering how long it will take to reach one million users requires more work in Step 3, so it is trickier but the procedure is the same. We still convert the info given into values for our variables, if there are one million users then \(U = 1000\text{,}\) and substitute that value into our formula:
    \begin{equation*} 1000 = 45(1.037)^t. \end{equation*}
    But, notice that this time, the unknown variable is not by itself on one side, so we need to solve this equation for \(t\text{.}\) Since the unknown is in the exponent, we’ll need logarithms to solve this equation. See Section 2.6 for a refresher.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} 1000 \amp = 45(1.037)^t\\ \frac{1000}{45} \amp = \frac{45(1.037)^t}{45}\\ \frac{200}{9} \amp = 1.037^t\\ \log\left(\frac{200}{9}\right) \amp = \log\left(1.037^t\right)\\ \log\left(\frac{200}{9}\right) \amp = t\log\left(1.037\right)\\ \frac{\log\left(\frac{200}{9}\right)}{\log(1.037)} \amp = t\\ 85.35 \amp \approx t \end{align*}
    🔗
    Since \(t\) is measured in months, this means that sometime during the 85th month after going public, the number of users will cross the one million mark. We can get a better idea of what this means with some unit conversion. There are 12 months in a year. Dividing 85 by 12 results in 7.08333. Seven years is \(7 \times 12 = 84\)months, leaving one month leftover. So, the site reaches one million users in March after being public for just over seven years.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Exponential growth is typically a good model for the spread of something, like the use of a new social networking website, or a disease, or a rumor, …. But, this stops being a good fit at some point. That growth cannot continue forever, because we run out of people! A graph of exponential growth becomes steeper and steeper. In the example above, the time it takes to reach another one million users will not be as long, less than two more years. Something like this tends to slow down when it has reached half of the maximum. If that is potential users of a networking site in a particular field of work, then the maximum is the number of people employed in the field. If we’re considering the spread of a disease, the max may be the entire world population. A rumor through a highschool, the max is the total number of students at the school.
